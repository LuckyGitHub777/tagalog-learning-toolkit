# Content Notes

## Editorial Method

The public package contains neutral, newly structured lesson materials rather than branded source documents. The lesson remains narrow: greetings, feelings, introductions, school, work, family, and simple descriptions.

## Language Decisions

1. **Kumusta / Kamusta** - `Kumusta?` is the primary study form. `Kamusta?` is noted as common everyday spelling.
2. **Okay / Ayos** - `Okay lang ako.` and `Ayos lang ako.` are treated as complete alternatives.
3. **Stress** - `Nai-stress ako.` is the primary form. `Stressed ako.` is accepted as familiar Taglish.
4. **Field of study** - `Nag-aaral ako ng ____.` is used as a natural foundational pattern for a subject or field.
5. **Occupation** - `____ ako.` is used with a profession, with `Ang trabaho ko ay ____.` retained as an alternative pattern.
6. **Kuya and Ate** - taught as older brother and older sister, with a note that both can be respectful forms of address.
7. **Tiyo/Tiya and Tito/Tita** - both formal-standard and common conversational variants are recognized.
8. **Bunso** - defined as the youngest child or sibling, not any younger sibling.
9. **Description pattern** - `Adjective + si + person` is the production pattern; `Si person ay adjective` is included for recognition.
10. **Nasasabik** - explained as excitement involving anticipation.

## Scope Control

The lesson does not attempt to teach full verb aspect, focus systems, linker rules, pronoun systems, politeness strategy, or regional variation. Those belong in later reviewed lessons.

## Review Questions Before Formal Classroom Adoption

- Should the course title use `Tagalog`, `Filipino`, or both for the intended audience?
- Which regional and register conventions should govern accepted answers?
- Which Taglish forms should be accepted, recognized only, or excluded?
- Should punctuation and spelling variants be scored strictly or flexibly?
- Which example names and places best represent the target learner population?
